What Is a 401k and How Does It Work?

📅 Updated June 2025 · ⏱ 7 min read · 👤 Beginner-friendly

A 401k is the most powerful retirement savings tool most Americans have access to — and millions of people either don't use it or don't use it correctly. Here's everything you need to know in plain English.

The Quick Answer

How a 401k Actually Works

When you enroll in your employer's 401k plan, a percentage of your paycheck goes directly into your 401k account before taxes are taken out. That money gets invested — usually in a mix of stock and bond funds you choose from a menu your employer provides.

The tax benefit is immediate: if you earn $60,000 and contribute $6,000 to your 401k, you only pay income tax on $54,000. That's a real tax saving of $600–$1,320 depending on your tax bracket.

The money then grows tax-deferred — meaning you don't pay taxes on gains until you withdraw the money in retirement. By that time, you may be in a lower tax bracket.

The Employer Match — Free Money You Can't Ignore

Many employers match a percentage of your 401k contributions. This is the single best financial benefit most employees never fully use.

Example: 50% match up to 6% of salary

Your salary$60,000/year
You contribute 6% ($3,600)$3,600/year
Employer adds 50% match$1,800/year FREE
Total going into your 401k$5,400/year

Not contributing enough to get the full match is like turning down a 50% raise on that portion of your salary. Always contribute at least enough to get the full employer match — before any other investing.

Traditional 401k vs Roth 401k

Traditional 401k

Tax now → Tax later
  • Contributions reduce taxable income today
  • Pay taxes when you withdraw in retirement
  • Better if you expect lower taxes in retirement
  • Required minimum distributions at 73

Roth 401k

Tax now → Tax free later
  • Contributions made with after-tax dollars
  • Withdrawals in retirement are tax-free
  • Better if you expect higher taxes in retirement
  • No required minimum distributions

If you're early in your career and currently in a lower tax bracket, the Roth 401k is often the better choice — you pay taxes now at a low rate, and all future growth is completely tax-free. If you're in a high tax bracket now, the Traditional 401k saves you more in taxes today.

What to Invest In

Your 401k offers a menu of investment options — usually mutual funds or index funds. For most people the best approach is simple:

  • Target-date fund — pick the fund closest to your expected retirement year (e.g. "2055 Fund"). It automatically adjusts from aggressive to conservative as you age. This is the best option for most beginners.
  • Index funds — low-cost funds that track the total stock market. Lower fees than actively managed funds. See our guide on what index funds are.
  • Avoid — high-fee actively managed funds. Check the expense ratio — anything above 0.5% is too expensive.

How Much Should You Contribute?

  1. At minimum: contribute enough to get the full employer match
  2. Ideally: contribute 10–15% of your salary total
  3. Maximum: $23,500/year in 2025

If you can't afford 10–15% right now, start with whatever gets you the full employer match and increase by 1% each year — or every time you get a raise. Small consistent increases compound dramatically over 20–30 years.

💡 The Most Important Thing to Do Today If your employer offers a 401k and you're not enrolled, enroll today. If you're enrolled but not getting the full employer match, increase your contribution to get it. These two actions take 15 minutes and could be worth $100,000+ over your career. Nothing else you do with money today has a higher return.

What Happens to Your 401k if You Leave Your Job?

You have four options when you leave an employer:

  • Leave it in the old employer's plan — simple, but you lose access to new contributions
  • Roll it over to your new employer's 401k — keeps everything consolidated
  • Roll it over to an IRA — more investment options, often lower fees
  • Cash it out — almost always a bad idea. You pay income taxes plus a 10% early withdrawal penalty

The rollover to an IRA is usually the best option — it gives you more investment choices and typically lower fees than employer plans.

Cómo funciona realmente un 401k

Cuando te inscribes en el plan 401k de tu empleador, un porcentaje de tu cheque de pago va directamente a tu cuenta 401k antes de que se descuenten los impuestos. Ese dinero se invierte — generalmente en una combinación de fondos de acciones y bonos que tú eliges de un menú que proporciona tu empleador.

El beneficio fiscal es inmediato: si ganas $60,000 y contribuyes $6,000 a tu 401k, solo pagas impuesto sobre la renta en $54,000.

¿En qué invertir?

Para la mayoría de las personas la mejor estrategia es simple:

  • Fondo de fecha objetivo — elige el fondo más cercano a tu año de retiro esperado (ej. "Fondo 2055"). Se ajusta automáticamente de agresivo a conservador a medida que envejeces.
  • Fondos indexados — fondos de bajo costo que rastrean el mercado bursátil total. Lee nuestra guía sobre qué son los fondos indexados.

¿Cuánto deberías contribuir?

  1. Como mínimo: contribuye suficiente para obtener el aporte completo del empleador
  2. Idealmente: contribuye el 10–15% de tu salario total
  3. Máximo: $23,500/año en 2025
💡 Lo más importante que puedes hacer hoy Si tu empleador ofrece un 401k y no estás inscrito, inscríbete hoy. Si estás inscrito pero no obtienes el aporte completo del empleador, aumenta tu contribución. Estas dos acciones toman 15 minutos y podrían valer $100,000+ durante tu carrera.
